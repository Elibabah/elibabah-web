# Translation glossary — *Imagination as Salvation in Juan Carlos Onetti's* A Brief Life

Working terminology for the English edition. One fixed rendering per term, held across all
four chapters. Nothing here is translated fresh where a discipline already has a settled
English word for it.

**Status: approved by Elías, 2026-08-22.** Binding for the whole English edition.

Decisions closed on approval:

- The part title keeps `imaginario que imagina` even though the Parra poem it echoes is cut.
- `angustia` → **anxiety** everywhere, technical and literary alike. No split.
- All epigraphs, front matter and chapter-level, are kept and translated.
- `grado ontológico` → **ontological order**.

---

## Governing principle

The thesis argues through Bachelard (65 mentions), Heidegger (45), Ricoeur, Sartre,
Castoriadis and Lukács. Those authors have **established English terminologies**. Inventing
fresh renderings for `modo de ser impropio` or `ser-en-el-mundo` would read as amateurish to
any English reader who knows the sources, and would sever the argument from the tradition it
is standing in.

So: where a term is borrowed from a theorist, we use that theorist's standard English. Where
the term is Elías's own, we choose once and hold it.

## 4. The `el ser` rule

`el ser` runs 141 times and does not mean one thing.

- **being** (lowercase) — the ontological sense: *el pozo del ser* → the well of being;
  *los viajes del ser* → the journeys of being; *la profundidad del ser* → the depth of being.
  This is the default and covers most occurrences.
- **his being / one's being** — where a possessive is implied: *lo íntimo del ser*,
  *la complejidad de su ser*.
- **beings** — plural, concrete: *todos los seres* → all beings.
- **Being** (capitalised) — reserve strictly for direct reference to Heidegger's *Sein*,
  as in the title *Being and Time*. Nowhere else.

Never "the Being". Never "essence".

## 6. Quotation policy

Two different rules, and the distinction is deliberate.

- **Onetti's novel** — always our own rendering, never Hortense Carpentier's published
  translation. These quotations are extensive and central, which is exactly where reproducing
  a published translation stops being quotation.
  - **Block quotations**: bilingual. Spanish first, English beneath.
  - **Short inline fragments**: English only, keeping the parenthetical page number.
    There are 240 of them across the thesis, woven into Elías's own sentences; bracketing
    the Spanish beside each would double the length and bury the argument. Nothing is lost,
    because the complete Spanish edition ships alongside as its own PDF.
    *(Decided 2026-08-22.)*
  - Elías's own emphases inside quotations (per his note 2, all italics in quotations are
    his) must be carried into the English.
- **Theory (Heidegger, Bachelard, Kundera, Lukács…)** — short attributed fragments may use
  the standard published English. Quotation right covers them, and rendering a famous phrase
  our own way would read as an error rather than as fidelity.
- **Spanish-language critics** — rendered into English, marked `[my translation]` on first
  use.
- **Bibliography** — source titles stay in their original language, always. This is the
  standing exception to the no-mixed-languages rule.

### `publicidad`: the pun English loses

Elías's argument in ch. 1 turns on a double sense Spanish has and English does not.
`publicidad` is both **advertising** (Macleod Publicidad) and Heidegger's **Öffentlichkeit**,
**publicness**. The move from the advertising agency to *das Man* is licensed by that single
word. Rendered "advertising" for the industry and "publicness" for Heidegger, with a
translator's note at ch. 1 n. 48 so the English reader can see why the leap is available.

Same category, smaller: `in-útil` (ch. 1) hyphenates *útil* to expose Heidegger's *útil*,
equipment. Rendered "un-useful" to keep the seam visible.

## Open questions for Elías

1. **`imaginario que imagina`** — the part title echoes Parra's *El hombre imaginario*, which
   we are cutting. Once the poem is gone the allusion has nothing to lean on for an English
   reader. Keep the phrasing anyway, or rework the part title?
2. **`angustia`** — technical uses take *anxiety* per Heidegger. Where the register is
   literary rather than philosophical, do you want *anguish*, or one word everywhere?
3. **Chapter epigraphs** — there are more beyond the front matter (Silvio Mattoni opens
   ch. 3, Milosz opens the introduction). Same treatment as the front-matter epigraphs:
   keep and translate?
4. **`grado ontológico`** — I propose *ontological order*. *Degree* is more literal but reads
   as a quantity, which is not what you mean. Confirm.
