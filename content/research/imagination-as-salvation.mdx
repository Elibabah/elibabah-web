I spent five years reading a single novel closely enough to write this, and it is still the
work that taught me how to read anything at all.

*La vida breve* opens on a man standing naked in a bathroom, listening through a wall to a
conversation he cannot quite hear, and filling the gaps with invention. Brausen imagines
because he cannot know. Out of that first involuntary act he builds a city, Santa María, and
then a second man to live in it; and the thesis asks what that building is for. My answer is
that it is salvation, in a strictly ontological sense: not rescue from his debts or his
marriage, but the recovery of a self he had let fall into forgetting.

The argument runs through three terms that turn out to be made of the same material. The trap
is not the wife, the job or the money; it is the human condition those things merely express.
Imagination is not escape but the opposite, a way of entering existence to its depths. And
salvation, if it exists at all, is found inside the trap rather than outside it, which is why
the city Brausen builds to save himself eventually burns.

The reading leans on Bachelard's phenomenology of imagination and Heidegger's account of
authentic and inauthentic being, with Lukács, Ricoeur, Sartre and Castoriadis at the edges. It
was written in Spanish, in an ornate academic register I have spent the years since learning to
carry into other kinds of writing. The English edition is a translation of that original, made
for readers who do not read Spanish; nothing has been added to the argument.

The distance between this and the work I do now is smaller than it looks. Reading a novel for
how it holds together, where the argument breaks, why one arrangement works and another does
not, turns out to be the same attention a system asks for.
