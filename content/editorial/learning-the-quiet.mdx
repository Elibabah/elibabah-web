My first time in the South Island taught me to listen before it taught me anything else. I had arrived expecting scenery, the postcard of mountains, and I found instead a silence that asks something of you; a silence that is not empty but full, the way a held breath is full. The landscape here does not perform. It waits.

I keep returning to that first morning. Winter was ending, but it felt more real than spring. Frost on the glass, the light hitting the naked trees, and no sound at all except my own uncertainty. The evenings closed over with rain, as if the water needed to make the atmosphere tangible.

And the wind deserves a whole chapter, as if the sky were fighting battles of its own. Below it, on the ground, my hope and I, and the family I brought with me, watching a new sun come up all the same.

Nobody tells you that moving to the far edge of the world is less an arrival than a kind of undressing; you leave behind your language, your weather, the face the mirror expects of you, and you stand in the cold as someone not yet finished. And yet the cold is honest. It does not pretend you belong before you do.

What is home, if not the place that agrees to wait while you become someone who can live in it? I am still learning the answer. The mountains, at least, are patient teachers.

This is Invercargill, and here my journey started. Now, not too far and not too close, I welcome the one who arrived a couple of months ago.
